Help Save the Outdoor Action Frosh Trip

On October 27th, Princeton University announced that it is cancelling all future Outdoor Action orientation backpacking trips. For more than 50 years, OA has welcomed students to Princeton and has been instrumental in building friendships, belonging, and a positive campus culture. We need your voice to help save Frosh Trip.

Organized independently by Princeton alumni. This campaign is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or coordinated with Outdoor Action or its staff. Please don't bother OA staff or leaders about it.

What happened

The University has announced that beginning with the 2027–28 academic year, first-year Orientation will be cut by four days, to eight, and Outdoor Action (along with the similar small-group programs Community Action and Dialogue and Difference in Action) will no longer be part of it. Instead, the University plans optional programs during fall break, the details of which are apparently still being worked out.

For OA, this represents an effective cancellation of the Frosh Trip, as many of the primary goals of the program depend on reaching students before they establish a rhythm on campus. An orientation program that takes place two months into the Princeton experience is no orientation program at all—it is a backpacking club.

By way of explanation, the University cited budgetary considerations and feedback about the length of orientation. That same week, the Daily Princetonian reported that the endowment had just reached an all-time high of $42.3 billion, and USG said it was not involved in the decision.

What we're asking for

Keep OA in orientation, before classes begin. At minimum, pause the change and consult students, OA leaders and alumni before taking apart a program that took over fifty years to build.

Source: The Daily Princetonian, Oct. 27, 2026. More coverage below.

What the research says

OA has been studied, and so have outdoor orientation programs like it. The findings are encouraging, and we've noted each study's limits so you can cite them with confidence.

The OA trip corrected first-years' overestimates of campus drinking

Brian Wardwell, “The Effects of the Outdoor Action Frosh Trip on Freshmen's Adaptation to Princeton University: A Study of Pluralistic Ignorance,” Princeton senior thesis in psychology, 1999.

Before their trip, incoming students expected to go out drinking about 1.75 nights a week themselves, but guessed the typical Princeton student did so 4.26 nights a week. After the trip, their estimate of the typical student fell to 2.48 nights, while their own plans barely changed. The gap stayed small through April. Students waitlisted for OA and students with no pre-orientation program showed much smaller changes (p < .001). OA participants' sense of social fit also rose after the trip, while non-participants' fell over the year.

Limits: students chose whether to go, and follow-up samples were small. The author treats the social-fit result as tentative because waitlisted students showed a similar change.

Wilderness orientation participants reported more social support

Brent J. Bell, “Wilderness Orientation: Exploring the Relationship Between College Preorientation Programs and Social Support,” Journal of Experiential Education 29(2): 145–167, 2006.

Among 1,601 first-years and sophomores at two colleges, students who did a wilderness orientation reported higher social support on all six measures of the Campus-Focused Social Provisions Scale than students in community-service programs, preseason athletics, or no program.

Limits: an exploratory study without controls for which students chose which program.

Outdoor trips put students on an equal social footing

Brent Bell, Mason Trumble and Renee Morrissette, “Social Status Equalization and Outdoor Orientation Programs: An Exploratory Study,” Journal of Experiential Education, 2020.

A survey of 4,484 students from 31 college outdoor orientation programs found that most participants felt equal in status to the rest of their group. That sense of equality was linked to being heard within the group and to shared living conditions on the trail, and students who felt it reported more positive experiences.

Limits: exploratory and based on self-reports.

The effects last for years

Michael A. Gass, Daniel E. Garvey and Deborah A. Sugerman, “The Long-Term Effects of a First-Year Student Wilderness Orientation Program,” Journal of Experiential Education 26(1): 34–40, 2003.

Seventeen years after a first-year wilderness orientation, participants still described lasting effects: rethinking assumptions about themselves and others, strong peer relationships formed on the trip, and benefits that carried through college and beyond.

Limits: a qualitative follow-up of participants from a single program.

Frequently asked questions

Who is behind this site?

We are a group of Princeton alumni who went on OA trips, and many of us served as Leaders, Leader Trainers, Support, or in other roles. This campaign is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or coordinated with Outdoor Action or staff, and none of them reviewed this site. Please don't bother them about it. Jim Valcourt ’12 made this website using Claude. Send questions or comments to saveprincetonoa@gmail.com.

What exactly is changing?

Starting with the 2027–28 academic year, Orientation will be four days shorter, and Outdoor Action, Community Action, and Dialogue and Difference in Action will no longer be part of it. For OA, that amounts to an effective cancellation of the Frosh Trip. The University plans to offer optional, free programs during fall break instead, with registration opening early in the semester. Details are reportedly still being worked out. The first class affected will arrive in fall 2027.

Isn't a fall-break trip just as good?

We don't think so. The OA Frosh Trip can introduce students to Princeton and create a magical atmosphere precisely because it happens before students fall into the rhythm of classes, activities, and the Street. A fall break trip will serve as a backpacking club, not an orientation, and a rescheduled trip will also reach fewer students.

The University says this is about budget. Isn't that reasonable?

The University's FAQ cites budgetary considerations, along with feedback that Orientation is long. The reality is that OA is a tiny fraction of the yearly budget and is led almost entirely by volunteers. If cost is truly the issue, alumni would welcome a conversation about how to help, including perhaps a return to a pre-2016 model where OA operates quasi-independently.

What about Community Action and DDA?

Most of us came through OA, so that's what this site focuses on. The same reasoning likely applies to all three small-group programs, though we aren’t familiar enough with the details for CA and DDA to speak for them.

I'm not an alum. Can I add my name?

Yes. Current students, parents and families, faculty and staff, and friends of OA are all welcome. The form asks for your connection to Princeton so each name is labeled accurately.

What happens to my information?

Your name, class year, OA connection, and optional message of support appear publicly on this site. If you provide your email, we use it only to add you to our Google Group. To remove your name, send an email to saveprincetonoa@gmail.com.

Will calling and writing actually make a difference?

Decisions like this get revisited when administrators hear from many people, quickly, with specific reasons. Polite, personal messages from alumni across many class years are the best chance of effecting change. The details of the fall-break programs are still being worked out, which makes now the time to speak up.
